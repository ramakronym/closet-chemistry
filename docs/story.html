A PRODUCT EXPERIMENT BY ANU

Good style.
Thoughtful systems.

Can a recommender help you see familiar clothes differently—and give you a useful reason to trust its suggestions?

01 / THE PRODUCT QUESTION

Start with something personal.

Outfit inspiration is everywhere. Turning an item you already own into a workable outfit takes a different kind of help. Closet Chemistry begins with one piece, then considers the occasion, existing wardrobe, spending limit, and the user’s feedback.

The first release is designed for exploration: try the sample wardrobe immediately, or upload an actual item and describe it. Three different looks create room for discovery without an endless feed.

02 / THE DECISIONS THAT MATTER

Preferences are flexible.
Promises are not.

Ownership, spending limits, purchase count, and locks are hard constraints. Color coordination, silhouette balance, occasion suitability, and style preferences influence ranking. A new look should not quietly swap a locked piece or exceed the budget.

“My actual wardrobe” and the demo wardrobe are kept separate. Uploading a skirt tells us about that skirt, not everything else in someone’s closet. If the available pieces cannot make a complete outfit, the interface explains the gap instead of inventing possessions.

The first prototype used original garment illustrations. After testing it, Anu asked to make the catalog shoppable using actual products from her public LTK. The current edit contains 47 linked product records with real listing photos, paid shopping links, and source-listed prices where available. Unpriced pieces are eligible only when marked as owned; the demo explicitly provides sample ownership. It is a curated snapshot, not a live account connection or inventory feed. Ambiguous records, beauty items, and a suspiciously priced dress were excluded.

03 / RECOMMEND, LEARN, EXPLAIN

A little chemistry, made visible.

The engine first filters eligible items, builds outfit candidates, and rejects combinations that violate constraints. It then scores occasion suitability, color coordination, silhouette balance, explicit style signals, and spending preferences. The three looks are assembled together with a hard constraint: no companion item can appear twice. Only the starting piece repeats. When the wardrobe cannot support three complete, distinct looks, the app explains the gap.

“Love this” gives a small, bounded boost to companion colors and styles while retaining that look. Office, weekend, and dinner learn independently, and switching back restores the previously viewed looks when constraints have not changed. A single like cannot override the no-repeat rule. “Too bold” favors quieter combinations. “Too expensive” lowers the spending limit. Locks remain in force, and feedback can be undone. A reset removes preference signals without deleting the wardrobe.

Explanations come from the actual selected items and ranking rules. The app does not invent claims about physical fit, fabric quality, availability, or what a user owns.

04 / BUILDING WITH CHATGPT WORK

Product direction meets implementation.

Anu defined the concept, the required controls, and the two entry points: a fast demo and an uploaded personal item. ChatGPT Work translated those requirements into the interface, original vector catalog, constrained recommendation engine, local photo workflow, and test coverage.

This release demonstrates content-based recommendation and explicit preference adaptation. It does not run an LLM or vision model, and it does not claim collaborative filtering. Uploaded item attributes are confirmed by the user. A later vision service could propose those attributes while preserving correction and manual entry.

05 / ARCHITECTURE & PRIVACY

Small enough to understand.

catalog.js contains the original illustration fallback. ltk-products.js supplies the active 47-product catalog, original LTK links, photo references, and price timestamps. catalog-provenance.json records where the product data came from. recommender.js implements candidate generation, constraints, ranking, and explanations. preferences.js bounds explicit feedback weights. app.js manages interactions, wardrobe state, feedback, and photo handling.

Photos are resized in the browser and saved to local browser storage. No photo is sent to a server. Users can remove a single photo or clear all uploads. There is no account or cross-device sync. Clearing browser data removes the saved wardrobe.

The static application can be hosted on GitHub Pages without a visitor login. Fonts are requested from Google Fonts with local font fallbacks; the app does not include analytics. Product photos load from LTK’s public image CDN, and shopping links preserve Anu’s published paid links. LTK and retailers may apply their own tracking after a click.

06 / WHAT NEEDS VALIDATION

Useful outfits, not just passing code.

Automated checks cover purchase count, owned-only mode, budgets, locks, incomplete wardrobes, candidate uniqueness, preference effects, catalog provenance, and exact-cent spending totals. Those checks establish rule compliance, not styling quality.

Next: have people assess occasion suitability, whether the three looks feel distinct, whether feedback helps, and whether they would actually wear the results. Upload usability, phone layouts, and wardrobe-building friction also need real-world observation. No user-study results or conversion improvements are claimed.
